(Hard)

1.In electrolysis of NaCl, why is molten NaCl used for extracting sodium and not NaCl solution?

Answer: This is because the electrolysis of NaCl solution yields hydrogen gas, chlorine gas and NaOH. Sodium is obtained only when fused NaCl is electrolysed.

2.Iron does not rust when stored along with a drying agent. Explain.

Answer: A drying agent is a hygroscopic chemical substance that absorbs and retains water or other moisture from its surroundings. This means that iron will not be exposed to water, which therefore prevents rusting.

3.Explain why the iron pillar near Qutub Minar does not rust.

Answer: The iron used to forge the pillar has a significantly high concentration of phosphorus, which forms a protective layer of iron hydrogen phosphate hydrate, acting as a barrier against the atmosphere's oxygen and moisture.

4.Why is it difficult to store nitric acid in metal containers, but not in glass containers?

Answer: Nitric acid is a strong oxidising agent. If stored in metals it will react to form salt and nitrogen dioxide. Glass does not react with nitric acid and hence, nitric acid is stored in glass.

5. A reddish-brown metal “X” does not react with dilute acids but reacts with oxygen on heating. Identify X.

Answer: X - Copper

6.Dry caustic soda cannot conduct electricity but its molten/solution form can conduct electricity. Justify.

Answer: Dry caustic soda, a solid ionic compound NaOH, does not conduct electricity because its sodium ions Na+ and hydroxide ions OH- are held rigidly in place by strong electrostatic forces, preventing free movement. However, in its molten or dissolved (aqueous) form, the crystal lattice breaks down, and the ions become free to move. These mobile ions act as charge carriers, enabling the conduction of electricity.

7.Why do metals like sodium and potassium need to be stored in kerosene, while zinc or copper can be safely kept in air? Explain with reactions.

Answer: Sodium and potassium react violently with air (oxygen) and especially with moisture — they oxidise and/or react with water producing hydroxides and hydrogen gas (which can ignite). Example: 2Na + 2H2O ---> 2NaOH + H2

8.Aluminium is highly reactive, yet it is used to make kitchen utensils and food storage containers. Justify.

Answer: Aluminium reacts readily but immediately forms a thin, layer of aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) on exposure to air. This oxide layer is tightly bound and prevents further reaction. That protective oxide layer prevents corrosion and makes aluminium suitable for utensils.

9.Why do metals generally form cations and non-metals form anions?

Answer: Metals have low ionisation energies (few valence electrons, weak hold by nucleus) so they lose electrons easily to form positive ions (cations). Non-metals have high electronegativity and higher ionisation energies and tend to gain electrons to complete octets, forming negative ions (anions).

10.Why does aluminium not corrode further after forming its oxide layer, but iron continues to rust?

Answer: Aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) forms an adherent, compact, impermeable layer that sticks tightly to the metal and prevents oxygen/water from reaching underlying metal. Iron oxide (rust) is porous and flaky, so it does not protect the underlying metal — it allows continued ingress of oxygen and water and so corrosion continues.

11.Why does iron rust in the presence of both air and moisture but not in dry air or distilled water alone?

Answer: Rusting requires both oxygen and water(electrolyte). Moisture provides an ionic medium for electron transfer; oxygen acts as the oxidising agent. In dry air, insufficient electrolyte exists for the electrochemical corrosion process. In pure distilled water conductivity is low, so corrosion is slow; presence of dissolved salts or acids speeds rusting.

12.Why is calcium hydroxide solution (lime water) used to test for CO₂ gas?

Answer: Ca(OH)2 + CO2 ---> CaCO3 + H2O

CaCO3 is milky white in color and forms a colloid when dissolved in water. Hence, limewater turns milky when CO2 is passed through it.

13.Why is aluminium used in the thermite process to extract metals like iron from their oxides, even though aluminium is more expensive?

Answer: In thermite process, aluminium reduces iron(III) oxide to iron releasing very high temperatures locally — sufficient to produce molten iron — without an external heat source.

14.You have three metals X, Y, and Z. Metal X reacts with cold water vigorously, Y reacts only with steam, and Z does not react even with steam. Arrange them in order of reactivity and predict possible identities of X, Y, and Z.

Answer: X = K, Na

Y = Mg, Zn, Fe

Z = Cu, Ag, Au

15.In metallurgy, why is roasting done for sulphide ores, but calcination is done for carbonate ores?

Answer: Roasting is done in presence of air and provides oxygen to metal sulphides, which is required to convert them to metallic oxide and SO2. Whereas, carbonate is changed to oxide by loss of CO2, which occurs in the absence of air and when heated to a high temperature. Hence, roasting is not done on carbonates.

16.Why is electrolysis necessary for the extraction of sodium, but not for iron?

Answer: Sodium is a very reactive metal; its oxide or chloride is so stable that carbon reduction is not feasible. Sodium is produced by electrolysis of molten NaCl because electric current forces reduction.

17.Why is zinc used for electroplating(galvanisation) of iron?

Answer: Electroplating of iron uses a metal **more reactive** than iron so that it provides a layer of protection. Zinc corrodes preferentially and protects exposed iron even if coating is scratched.

18.A metal “M” reacts with dilute sulphuric acid to form salt MSO₄ and hydrogen gas. If M displaces zinc from ZnSO₄ solution, identify M.

Answer: M - Ca, Fe, Mg

19.An ore sample is black in colour. On heating in air it gives off a gas that turns lime water milky. Identify the type of ore and the process involved.

Answer: Carbonate ore.

20.You have four samples: metal A, metal B and their oxides (A₂O and B₂O). Experiments show:
